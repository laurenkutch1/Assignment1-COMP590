Assignment 1: Building and Deploying Your First VR App

This is a VR target shooting game for Google Cardboard. Using head tracking, the player looks around a simple 3D scene to find a bullseye target, then taps the screen to shoot a ball in whatever direction they're looking. Hitting the target updates a live score counter that floats above it in real time. The game uses head-tracked aiming, where the player's view direction determines the ball's path, so aiming means physically turning to face the target. It also uses physics-based projectiles, where each ball has a Rigidbody and is launched with a force so gravity affects its arc instead of it flying in a straight line. Lastly, for step 6 of the assignment, I added a score feedback loop, where a ScoreManager script tracks hits and updates a text display near the target to give the player immediate feedback. Referencing back to chapter 4 of The Art of Game Design, by Jesse Schell, a game can be understood as a problem-solving activity approached with a playful attitude. Schell arrives at this by pulling out a set of qualities that recurring definitions of "game" share, and my app maps onto several of them directly. First, the game has a clear goal. The problem the player is trying to solve is "how many times can I hit the target?" This is the objective the player sets out to answer. The game also contains rules. The player can only affect the outcome in one way by aiming their head and tapping to launch a ball. The ball's flight is then controlled by Unity's physics engine (gravity, force), not by direct player control. This constraint is exactly what Schell means by rules confining the player's actions. There is also a challenge/conflict when playing the game. Since the ball follows a physics-driven arc rather than snapping instantly to wherever the player is looking, landing a hit requires some judgment about timing and aim, which makes the activity genuine difficulty rather than a guaranteed outcome. There is also interactivity involved. The player isn't a passive observer and every shot is a direct action. The head-tracked aiming means the player has to physically engage with the space around them in order to play well and increase their score. Lastly, the game is a problem the player chooses to solve. The player is not forced to keep playing and can set their own goal, such as asking, “Can I beat my last score?” This makes the game more than just a physics simulation because the player is choosing to engage with the challenge. Overall, the game fits Schell’s definition because it gives the player a simple problem to solve, clear rules to follow, and enough challenge through the physics to make hitting the target worth trying.
